# 🌾 Grains: The Foundation of Spirits

Grains are the backbone of beer, whiskey, vodka, gin, and many other spirits. Understanding grain processing helps you appreciate what makes each drink unique.

## The Big Four Grains

### 🌾 Barley
**Used in**: Beer, Scotch, Irish whiskey, some vodkas

**Flavor profile**: Nutty, bready, slightly sweet  
**Starch content**: High (60-65%)  
**Why it matters**: Contains enzymes that convert its own starches to sugar

**Forms**:
- **Raw barley** — rarely used alone, needs external enzymes
- **Malted barley** — sprouted and dried, activates enzymes (see Malting Process below)
- **Roasted barley** — gives stouts their dark color and coffee notes

### 🌽 Corn (Maize)
**Used in**: Bourbon, Tennessee whiskey, most American whiskeys, some vodkas

**Flavor profile**: Sweet, mild, vanilla notes  
**Starch content**: Very high (70-75%)  
**Why it matters**: Cheapest grain, produces smooth, sweet spirits

**Forms**:
- **Flaked corn** — steamed and rolled, ready to mash
- **Cracked corn** — coarsely ground
- **Cornmeal** — finely ground

### 🌾 Rye
**Used in**: Rye whiskey, some bourbons, some vodkas

**Flavor profile**: Spicy, peppery, dry, bold  
**Starch content**: Moderate (55-60%)  
**Why it matters**: Adds complexity and bite to whiskey

**Challenge**: Creates thick, sticky mash (difficult to work with)

### 🌾 Wheat
**Used in**: Wheat beer, some bourbon (wheated bourbon), some vodkas

**Flavor profile**: Soft, smooth, slightly sweet, light  
**Starch content**: High (60-65%)  
**Why it matters**: Creates gentle, approachable spirits

---

## Grain Processing: From Field to Fermentation

### 1. MALTING (Barley only)
**Purpose**: Activate enzymes that convert starch → sugar

**Process**:
1. **Steep** — Soak barley in water 2-3 days (40-45°F)
   - Grain absorbs water, increases from 12% to 45% moisture
2. **Germinate** — Spread on floor or in drums for 4-6 days
   - Rootlets sprout, enzymes activate
   - Called "green malt" at this stage
3. **Kiln** — Dry in hot air to stop sprouting
   - Light kilning (122-149°F) = pale malt for light beers/whiskeys
   - Medium (167-185°F) = amber malt, toasted flavor
   - Heavy (392-428°F) = chocolate/black malt for stouts

**Result**: "Malted barley" or "malt" — the sugar-making powerhouse

**Why other grains skip this**: Barley has the best enzymes. Other grains can rely on barley's enzymes when mixed together, or distillers add commercial enzymes.

---

### 2. MILLING/CRUSHING
**Purpose**: Expose starch inside the grain for water access

**Methods**:

**CRUSHING** *(Beer)*
- Grain passes through rollers
- Cracks husk but keeps it mostly intact
- Husks act as natural filter during lautering
- Creates "grist" — mix of husk, grits, and flour

**GRINDING** *(Spirits)*
- Hammermills or roller mills pulverize grain into meal
- Finer grind = more surface area = more efficient extraction
- No need to preserve husks (spirits are distilled, not filtered)

**Particle sizes**:
- **Coarse** (flour) — 20% of malt
- **Grits** — 70% of malt  
- **Husks** — 10% of malt (intact)

**Why size matters**: Too fine = sticky mash, hard to drain; Too coarse = poor starch extraction

---

### 3. MASHING
**Purpose**: Convert grain starch into fermentable sugar

**The Chemistry**:
- Starch = long chains of glucose (insoluble in water)
- Enzymes break chains → glucose, maltose (fermentable sugars)
- Different temps activate different enzymes:
  - **145-158°F** = Beta-amylase → more fermentable sugars (dry beer/spirits)
  - **158-167°F** = Alpha-amylase → less fermentable sugars (sweeter, fuller beer)

**Process**:
1. **Mix crushed grain with hot water** (called "strike water")
   - Beer: 1.25-2 quarts water per pound of grain
   - Spirits: 2-4 quarts per pound (thinner mash, easier to work with)
   - Temperature: 145-158°F typically
2. **Hold temperature** for 60-90 minutes
   - Enzymes work their magic
   - Starch → sugar conversion happens
3. **Vorlauf** (beer only) — recirculate liquid until clear
4. **Lautering** (beer only) — drain sugary liquid through grain bed
5. **Sparging** (beer only) — rinse grains with hot water to extract remaining sugars

**Result**: 
- Beer → "wort" (pronounced "wert") — sweet, sugary liquid
- Spirits → "mash" or "distiller's beer" — ready for fermentation

**For spirits**: The whole mash (grain + liquid) goes into fermentation, then distillation. No filtering needed.

---

### 4. COOKING (Non-Malted Grains)
**Purpose**: Soften grain and gelatinize starch so enzymes can access it

**Why it's needed**: Corn, rye, and wheat have tough starch granules that don't dissolve at normal mashing temps

**Process**:
1. **Mix grain with water**
2. **Heat to 150-180°F** (depending on grain)
   - Corn: 165°F for 30-60 min
   - Rye: 150°F for 30 min
   - Wheat: 140-158°F for 30 min
3. **Cool to mashing temp** (145-158°F)
4. **Add malted barley** (for enzymes) OR commercial enzymes
5. **Hold for 60-90 min** while enzymes convert starch

**Bourbon example**:
- Typical mash bill: 70% corn, 15% rye, 15% malted barley
- Cook corn first (gelatinize)
- Cool down, add rye and malt
- Malt's enzymes convert ALL the starches to sugar

## Grain Flavor Contributions

Different grains give different character to the final product:

| Grain | What it brings |
|-------|----------------|
| **Corn** | Sweetness, vanilla, mild flavor — used in bourbon for smoothness |
| **Rye** | Spicy bite, peppery notes, dryness — backbone of rye whiskey |
| **Wheat** | Soft, gentle, smooth — makes "wheated" bourbon (like Maker's Mark) |
| **Barley** | Nutty, malty, bready — foundation of Scotch and beer |
| **Malted barley** | Same as barley but sweeter, richer — provides enzymes + flavor |

## Special Grain Techniques

### Sour Mashing (Whiskey)
- Save portion of previous mash (like sourdough starter)
- Adds to new mash for consistency
- Lowers pH, protects against bacteria
- Creates continuity batch-to-batch
- **Note**: "Sour mash" whiskey isn't sour-tasting, it's a process

### Cereal Cooking (Whiskey)
- Cook adjunct grains (corn/rye) separately at high temp
- Transfer to main mash with malted barley
- Allows each grain to be processed at optimal temperature

### Turbid Mashing (Belgian Beer)
- Deliberately create cloudy, starchy wort
- Leaves some unfermented starches
- Food for wild yeast/bacteria in spontaneous fermentation
- Used in lambics, gueuze

---

## From Grain to Glass: Quick Comparison

### BEER
Malt grain → Mill (crack) → Mash with water (145-158°F) → Drain wort → Boil with hops → Ferment → Beer

### WHISKEY
Mix grains → Cook (if corn/rye) → Add malt → Mash → Ferment WHOLE mash → Distill → Age in barrels → Whiskey

### VODKA
Grain → Grind → Cook/Mash → Ferment → Distill MANY times → Filter → Dilute → Vodka

**Key difference**: Beer makers drain liquid and discard grain. Spirit makers ferment and distill everything together.

---

## Quick Grain Facts

**Most efficient grain**: Barley (has its own enzymes)  
**Highest starch**: Corn (75%)  
**Hardest to work with**: Rye (sticky, thick mash)  
**Smoothest**: Wheat (gentle, no bite)  
**Most versatile**: Malted barley (enzymes work on ALL grains)

**Why bourbon is mostly corn**: It's cheap, abundant in America, and produces a sweet, smooth whiskey that ages well.

**Why Scotch is all barley**: Tradition, terroir, and barley grows well in Scotland's climate.

# 🧪 Fermentation: Where the Magic Happens

Fermentation transforms sugar into alcohol. Understanding it helps you appreciate why a pilsner tastes crisp, a hefeweizen tastes like banana, and bourbon gets its character.

## What is Fermentation?

**In plain English**: Yeast eats sugar, produces alcohol + CO₂ + flavor

**The science**: Yeast (single-celled fungus) metabolizes sugar without oxygen, creating ethanol, carbon dioxide, and heat as byproducts

**The equation**: C₆H₁₂O₆ (sugar) → 2 C₂H₅OH (ethanol) + 2 CO₂

---

## Meet the Yeast

### 🦠 Ale Yeast *(Saccharomyces cerevisiae)*
**Temperature**: 60-75°F (warm)  
**Location**: Top-fermenting (floats on surface)  
**Flavor**: Fruity esters (banana, apple, pear), spicy phenols  
**Used in**: Ales, wine, whiskey, sake  
**Personality**: Fast, aggressive, flavorful

### 🦠 Lager Yeast *(Saccharomyces pastorianus)*
**Temperature**: 45-55°F (cold)  
**Location**: Bottom-fermenting (sinks to bottom)  
**Flavor**: Clean, crisp, subtle  
**Used in**: Lagers, pilsners  
**Personality**: Slow, patient, neutral

### 🦠 Wild Yeast & Bacteria
**Brettanomyces** (Brett)
- Funky, earthy, farmhouse character
- Leather, barnyard, tropical fruit
- Used in: Sour beers, farmhouse ales

**Lactobacillus**
- Sour, tart (same bacteria in yogurt)
- Clean acidity
- Used in: Berliner weisse, gose

**Pediococcus**
- More sourness, butter notes
- Works slowly (months)
- Used in: Lambics, Flanders red ales

### 🍄 Koji Mold *(Aspergillus oryzae)*
**Not yeast** — but critical for sake/shochu  
**What it does**: Converts rice starch → sugar while yeast ferments simultaneously  
**Result**: Parallel fermentation (unique to sake)

## The Four Phases of Fermentation

### Phase 1: LAG (0-24 hours)
**Yeast is**: Waking up, multiplying, preparing

**What you see**: Nothing — calm liquid

**What's happening**:
- Yeast absorbs oxygen
- Builds cell walls
- Population grows
- Acclimates to temperature

**Critical**: Right temp, healthy yeast, good oxygen levels

---

### Phase 2: EXPONENTIAL GROWTH (1-4 days)
**Yeast is**: Working overtime, making alcohol

**What you see**:
- Heavy bubbling
- Thick foam (krausen) on top
- Airlock going crazy
- Temperature rising

**What's being made**:
- Alcohol (ethanol)
- CO₂ (bubbles)
- Flavor compounds (esters, phenols)
- Heat

**Most critical phase**: Temperature control makes or breaks flavor

---

### Phase 3: STATIONARY (3-7 days)
**Yeast is**: Running out of sugar, cleaning up

**What you see**:
- Bubbling slows way down
- Foam collapses
- Liquid starts clearing

**What's happening**:
- Final sugar → alcohol
- Yeast reabsorbs off-flavors
- Yeast settles to bottom

---

### Phase 4: CONDITIONING (days to years)
**Yeast is**: Dormant, job done

**What you see**:
- Clear liquid
- Sediment layer
- No activity

**Timeline by drink**:
- Beer: 1-4 weeks (carbonation, smoothing)
- Spirits: Done (ready to distill)
- Wine: Months to years (complexity develops)

---

## Temperature: The Flavor Controller

### ❄️ TOO COLD
*(Below 50°F for ales, 40°F for lagers)*

**What happens**:
- Yeast goes dormant
- Stuck fermentation
- Incomplete conversion
- Clean but weak

---

### ✅ JUST RIGHT

**Ales**: 60-72°F
- 60-65°F = cleaner, crisper
- 68-72°F = fruitier, more esters

**Lagers**: 45-55°F
- Slow fermentation (2-3x longer)
- Crisp, clean finish
- Minimal off-flavors

**Whiskey**: 70-85°F
- Warmer = faster, more flavor compounds
- Cooler = cleaner, slower

---

### 🔥 TOO WARM
*(Above 80°F for ales, 60°F for lagers)*

**What happens**:
- Fusel alcohols (hot, solvent taste)
- Excessive esters (nail polish, banana bomb)
- Stressed yeast
- Fast but flawed

---

## How Dry vs. Sweet: Attenuation

**Attenuation** = percentage of sugar the yeast eats

### High Attenuation (75-85%)
- Dry finish
- Higher ABV
- Light body
- Examples: Brut champagne, saison, IPA

### Low Attenuation (65-75%)
- Sweet finish
- Fuller body
- Lower ABV (same starting sugar)
- Examples: Sweet wine, milk stout, Scottish ale

**Controlled by**:
- Yeast strain
- Temperature
- Sugar complexity
- Nutrients

---

## Fermentation Vessels

### Open Fermentation
**Setup**: Open to air (cloth cover)

**Benefits**:
- Heat escapes easily
- Easy to skim yeast
- Traditional flavors

**Risks**:
- Wild yeast contamination
- Oxidation
- Bugs

**Used for**: Traditional British ales, some Belgians, wine

---

### Closed Fermentation
**Setup**: Sealed vessel + airlock (CO₂ out, air blocked)

**Benefits**:
- No contamination
- No oxidation
- Full control
- Capture CO₂

**Drawback**: 
- Heat harder to manage

**Used for**: Most modern beer, all spirits, most wine

---

## Primary vs. Secondary

### Primary Fermentation
**What**: Main alcohol production

**Duration**:
- Beer: 3-7 days
- Wine: 1-3 weeks
- Spirits: 3-7 days

**Done when**: Gravity stable, bubbling stopped, clearing

---

### Secondary Fermentation
**What**: Conditioning, clarification, extra steps

**Duration**:
- Beer: 1-4 weeks (optional)
- Wine: Months (aging)
- Champagne: In-bottle (months to years)

**Why**:
- Crystal clear liquid
- Yeast drops out
- Off-flavors cleaned
- Dry hopping, fruit additions

**Skip for**: Spirits (distilled anyway), quick beers

---

## Special Techniques

### Spontaneous Fermentation
**No yeast added** — wild yeast from air does the work

**Process**:
1. Expose wort to open air overnight
2. Wild yeast/bacteria colonize
3. Fermentation takes 1-3 years
4. Unpredictable results

**Used in**: Belgian lambics, some natural wines  
**Flavor**: Funky, sour, barnyard, complex

---

### Mixed Fermentation
**Multiple organisms** work together or in sequence

**Common combo**:
- Ale yeast (primary)
- Add Brettanomyces (funk)
- Add Lactobacillus (sour)

**Used in**: Sour beers, farmhouse ales  
**Timeline**: Months to years

---

### Stuck Fermentation
**Problem**: Yeast quits early

**Causes**:
- Too cold
- Starved (no nutrients)
- Too much alcohol (yeast dies)
- Too little yeast pitched
- No oxygen

**Fixes**:
- Warm it up
- Add nutrients
- Fresh yeast
- Rouse settled yeast

---

### Champagne Method *(Méthode Champenoise)*
**Secondary fermentation in the bottle**

**Process**:
1. Make still wine
2. Add sugar + yeast to bottle
3. Cork and wait (months to years)
4. Yeast creates CO₂ (trapped = bubbles)
5. Riddling (tilt bottles, collect sediment in neck)
6. Disgorgement (freeze neck, pop out plug)
7. Top off + final cork

**Result**: Fine, persistent bubbles  
**Time**: 15 months minimum (vintage = 3-7 years)

---

## Flavor Byproducts: What Yeast Creates

### 🍌 Esters (Fruity)
**What**: Alcohols + acids combine

**Flavors**:
- Banana (isoamyl acetate)
- Apple (ethyl hexanoate)
- Pear (ethyl acetate)
- Pineapple (ethyl butyrate)

**More esters from**: Warm temps, low oxygen, high sugar

**Common in**: Belgian ales, hefeweizens, English ales

---

### 🌶️ Phenols (Spicy)
**What**: Aromatic compounds

**Flavors**:
- Clove (4-vinyl guaiacol)
- Smoke
- Pepper
- Medicinal

**More phenols from**: Certain yeast strains, warm temps

**Common in**: Hefeweizens, Belgian ales, farmhouse ales

---

### 🔥 Fusel Alcohols (Hot)
**What**: Higher alcohols (more carbons than ethanol)

**Flavors**:
- Solvent
- Hot/burning
- Rose (phenylethanol)

**More fusels from**: High temps, stressed yeast, low oxygen

**Good**: Low levels add complexity  
**Bad**: High levels = harsh finish

---

### 🧈 Diacetyl (Butter)
**What**: Yeast metabolism byproduct

**Flavor**: Butter, butterscotch, slick mouthfeel

**Good in**: British ales (low levels), Czech pilsners  
**Bad in**: Most lagers, most ales (high levels)

**Avoided by**: Proper temps, diacetyl rest, healthy yeast

---

### 💨 Sulfur Compounds
**What**: Volatile sulfur

**Flavors**:
- Rotten eggs (H₂S)
- Struck match
- Cooked corn
- Onion/garlic

**Common in**: Lagers (usually blows off during conditioning)

**Caused by**: Yeast strain, stressed yeast, dying yeast

---

## When Yeast Gives Up: Alcohol Tolerance

| Yeast Type | Max ABV | Notes |
|------------|---------|-------|
| Standard ale | 8-12% | Gets "drunk," stops |
| Wine yeast | 12-16% | Alcohol toxicity |
| Champagne yeast | 16-18% | High tolerance |
| Distiller's yeast | 18-20% | Bred for spirits |
| Turbo yeast | 20-25% | Lab-created |

**At the limit**:
- Metabolism slows
- Cell walls break down
- Fermentation stops
- Remaining sugar = sweet finish

---

## Monitoring Fermentation

### Specific Gravity (SG)
**Measures**: Liquid density vs. water

**How it works**:
- Water = 1.000
- Sugar = heavier (1.050)
- Alcohol = lighter (1.010)
- Gravity drops as sugar → alcohol

**Calculate ABV**:
- Original Gravity (OG): 1.050
- Final Gravity (FG): 1.010
- ABV ≈ (1.050 - 1.010) × 131 = **5.2%**

**Tools**: Hydrometer or refractometer

---

### pH (Acidity)
**Sweet spots**:
- Beer wort: 5.2-5.6 pH
- Wine: 3.0-4.0 pH
- Whiskey mash: 5.0-5.5 pH

**Why it matters**:
- Too high = poor enzyme activity, harsh
- Too low = stressed yeast, stuck fermentation
- Low pH blocks bacteria (protection)

---

## Quick Fermentation Facts

**Fastest**: Ale at 70°F = 3-5 days  
**Slowest**: Lambic = 1-3 years  
**Most predictable**: Lager (cold + slow = clean)  
**Least predictable**: Wild/spontaneous  
**Hottest**: Bourbon mash hits 95°F+ from yeast heat  
**Coldest**: Ice wine ferments near freezing

**Why distillers ignore sediment**: It all gets distilled out  
**Why brewers obsess**: That sediment ends up in your glass

# 🍺 Beer
*4-12% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Water** (90%) + mineral content affects taste
- **Malted barley** → soaked, sprouted, dried grains that provide sugars
  - Pale malt = base, Crystal = caramel, Chocolate = dark roasted
- **Hops** → flowers that add bitterness, aroma (citrus/pine/floral), preservation
- **Yeast** → eats sugar, makes alcohol + flavor
  - Ale yeast (warm) = fruity, Lager yeast (cold) = crisp

### How it's made
Mash grains → boil with hops → ferment → age → bottle

### Types you'll see

**LAGERS** *(cold-fermented, crisp)*
- **Pilsner** — golden, hoppy, clean (Czech/German)
- **Helles/Bock** — maltier, breadier

**ALES** *(warm-fermented, fruity)*
- **IPA** — hop bomb, citrus/pine flavors
  - Hazy IPA = juicy, cloudy
  - Double IPA = stronger, more hops
- **Pale Ale** — balanced hops and malt
- **Stout** — black, roasted barley, coffee/chocolate
- **Porter** — like stout but lighter
- **Wheat Beer** — 30-70% wheat, smooth, cloudy
  - Hefeweizen = banana/clove (German)
  - Witbier = orange peel/coriander (Belgian)
- **Sour** — intentionally tart/acidic from wild yeast or bacteria

---

# 🍷 Wine
*9-16% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Grapes** — specific wine varieties (not eating grapes)
  - Skins = color + tannins, Juice = sugar + acid
- **Yeast** — wild or cultured
- **Oak barrels** (optional) — add vanilla, toast, spice

### How it's made
**Red**: crush grapes → ferment WITH skins → press → age  
**White**: press grapes → ferment WITHOUT skins → age  
**Rosé**: brief skin contact (hours) or blend red + white

### Grape Guide

**RED WINES**
- **Cabernet Sauvignon** — full-bodied, black currant, high tannins
- **Pinot Noir** — light, delicate, cherry, low tannins
- **Merlot** — medium, plum, soft tannins
- **Syrah/Shiraz** — bold, pepper, dark fruit
- **Zinfandel** — jammy berries, high alcohol
- **Malbec** — plum, chocolate, soft

**WHITE WINES**
- **Chardonnay** — versatile, unoaked = crisp apple, oaked = butter/vanilla
- **Sauvignon Blanc** — crisp, citrus, grass, high acid
- **Riesling** — dry to sweet, apple/peach, high acid
- **Pinot Grigio/Gris** — light and crisp OR rich (Alsace)

**SPARKLING**
- **Champagne** — France only, traditional method, fine bubbles
- **Prosecco** — Italian, tank method, lighter/fruitier
- **Cava** — Spanish, traditional method

**FORTIFIED** *(wine + brandy, 15-22% ABV)*
- **Port** — sweet Portuguese, Ruby = young, Tawny = aged/nutty
- **Sherry** — Spanish, Fino = dry, Pedro Ximénez = sweet/syrupy
- **Madeira** — Portuguese, heated, caramelized

---

# 🍶 Sake
*15-20% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Sake rice** (polished 30-50% away) — more polish = more refined
- **Water** (80%)
- **Koji mold** — converts starch → sugar
- **Yeast** — sugar → alcohol

### How it's made
Polish rice → steam → add koji → ferment (simultaneously converts starch AND ferments)

### Quality levels
More polishing = higher grade = more $$$
- Junmai = 70% remaining
- Ginjo = 60% remaining  
- Daiginjo = 50% remaining

---

# 🍎 Cider
*4-8% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Cider apples** — small, tannic, bitter (not eating apples)
  - Sweet, Bittersweet, Sharp, Bittersharp varieties
- **Yeast** — wild or cultured

### How it's made
Press apples → ferment → carbonate (optional)

---

# 🍯 Mead
*8-20% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Honey** (70-80% sugar) — type = flavor
  - Wildflower, Clover, Orange blossom, Buckwheat
- **Water** (3-4 parts water : 1 part honey)
- **Yeast**

### How it's made
Dilute honey → ferment (add nutrients since honey lacks them)

### Variations
- **Melomel** — fruit added
- **Metheglin** — spiced
- **Braggot** — mead + beer hybrid

---

# ⚪ Vodka
*40% ABV*

### What's in it?
Base (pick one): **Grains** (wheat/rye/corn) · **Potatoes** · **Grapes** · Sugar beets · Honey  
Plus: **Water** (60% of final product) · **Yeast**

### How it's made
Ferment → distill 3+ times → filter (charcoal) → dilute with water

**Goal**: Pure, neutral, flavorless spirit

---

# 🥃 Whiskey
*40-50% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Grains** (the "mash bill")
  - Corn = sweet, Rye = spicy, Barley = nutty, Wheat = soft
- **Water**
- **Yeast**
- **Charred oak barrels** — provides 50-70% of flavor (vanilla, caramel, oak)

### How it's made
Mash grains → ferment → distill → age in barrels (months to decades)

### Types by grain

**BOURBON** *(American)*
- 51%+ corn, aged in NEW charred oak
- Sweet: vanilla, caramel, toffee

**SCOTCH** *(Scotland)*
- 100% malted barley (single malt) OR grain blend
- 3+ years aging
- Islay = smoky/peaty, Speyside = fruity, Highland = full

**IRISH**
- Triple-distilled = smoother
- Malted + unmalted barley
- Light, honey, vanilla

**RYE**
- 51%+ rye grain
- Spicy, peppery, dry

**TENNESSEE**
- Like bourbon but filtered through maple charcoal
- Slightly smoother

**JAPANESE**
- Like Scotch, very refined
- Uses Mizunara oak (sandalwood notes)

**CANADIAN**
- Blended grains, smooth, light
- Often called "rye" regardless of content

# 🏴‍☠️ Rum
*40% ABV*

### What's in it?
Base (pick one): **Molasses** (most common) · **Fresh sugarcane juice** (agricole/cachaça)  
Plus: **Water** · **Yeast** (wild = funky, cultured = clean) · **Oak barrels** (for aged)

### How it's made
Ferment molasses/juice → distill → age (optional) → bottle

### Types by aging

**WHITE/SILVER**
- Unaged or filtered clear
- Light, subtle, vanilla notes
- Cocktails

**GOLD/AMBER**
- 1-3 years in oak
- Caramel, vanilla, smooth

**DARK**
- 5+ years OR added molasses
- Rich, molasses, toffee, spice

**SPICED**
- Infused with cinnamon, vanilla, nutmeg

**OVERPROOF**
- 50-75% ABV
- Extra strong

**AGRICOLE**
- Fresh cane juice (not molasses)
- Grassy, vegetal, funky
- French Caribbean

# 🌵 Tequila
*40% ABV*

### What's in it?
- **Blue agave** plant hearts (7-10 years to mature)
  - Cooked to convert starch → sugar
- **Water**
- **Yeast**
- **Oak barrels** (for aged types)

### How it's made
Harvest agave → cook hearts → crush → ferment → distill

⚠️ **100% agave** = quality | "Mixto" (51% agave) = avoid

### Types by aging

**BLANCO/SILVER**
- Unaged, clear
- Pure agave flavor: crisp, peppery, herbaceous

**REPOSADO** *(rested)*
- 2-12 months in oak
- Pale gold, smooth, vanilla hints

**AÑEJO** *(aged)*
- 1-3 years in oak
- Amber, complex, butterscotch, oak

**EXTRA AÑEJO**
- 3+ years
- Dark amber, ultra-smooth, like whiskey

**CRISTALINO**
- Aged then filtered clear
- Smooth taste, clear look

# 📊 Quick Reference

## Fermented vs. Distilled

| | Fermented | Distilled |
|---|---|---|
| **Process** | Yeast eats sugar → alcohol | Heat fermented liquid → collect alcohol vapor |
| **ABV** | 3-20% | 40%+ |
| **Examples** | Beer, wine, sake | Vodka, whiskey, gin |

## Proof = ABV × 2
100 proof = 50% ABV  
80 proof = 40% ABV

## Aging
**What**: Spirit sits in oak barrels  
**Why**: Adds color, vanilla/caramel flavor, mellows harshness  
**Where**: Bourbon = new barrels, Scotch/rum = used bourbon barrels

# 📦 Storage & Shelf Life

Proper storage keeps your alcohol tasting great. Some bottles last forever, others need careful handling.

## The General Rules

### 🌡️ Temperature
**Cool & consistent** is key
- Ideal: 55-65°F
- Avoid: Direct sunlight, heat sources, temperature swings
- Hot attics/garages = flavor degradation
- Freezer = OK for vodka/gin, NOT for wine

### 📍 Position
**Bottles with corks**: Store horizontal (keeps cork moist)  
**Bottles with screw caps**: Upright is fine  
**Opened bottles**: Usually upright

### 💡 Light
**Dark is better**
- UV light degrades flavor
- Brown/green bottles protect better than clear
- Wine cellars are dark for a reason

---

## Unopened Bottles: Shelf Life

### ♾️ LASTS FOREVER
**Spirits 40%+ ABV** (vodka, gin, rum, whiskey, tequila, brandy)
- Alcohol preserves itself
- Flavor stays stable for decades
- Exception: If poorly sealed, alcohol evaporates

**Why**: High alcohol = antiseptic, nothing can grow

---

### 📅 LASTS YEARS (but not forever)

**Liqueurs** *(1-2 years unopened, varies by type)*
- Cream liqueurs (Baileys): 2 years max
- High-proof (Chartreuse): 5+ years
- Fruit liqueurs: 1-2 years
- Check bottle for date

**Beer** *(3-6 months for most, longer for some)*
- IPAs: Drink fresh (hops fade fast)
- Lagers/Pilsners: 6 months
- Stouts/Porters: 1 year
- Barleywines/Belgian strong: 5+ years (improves with age)
- Pasteurized lasts longer than unpasteurized

**Cider** *(6 months to 1 year)*
- Similar to beer
- Hard cider: 6-9 months

---

### 🍷 DEPENDS ON THE WINE

**Most wines**: 1-3 years
- Drink young (not meant for aging)
- White wines: 1-2 years
- Light reds: 2-3 years

**Age-worthy wines**: 5-20+ years
- Bordeaux, Burgundy, Barolo
- Vintage port
- High-end Napa Cab
- Needs proper cellar conditions

**Sparkling** *(Champagne/Prosecco)*
- Non-vintage champagne: 3-4 years
- Vintage champagne: 10+ years
- Prosecco: 1-2 years (drink young)

**Fortified** *(Port, Sherry, Madeira)*
- Unopened: 2-20+ years depending on type
- Vintage port: Decades
- Tawny port: Drink on purchase
- Madeira: Can outlast you

---

## Opened Bottles: Use By

### 🥃 SPIRITS (40%+ ABV)
**Timeline**: 1-2 years, often longer

**What happens**: 
- Alcohol slowly evaporates
- Oxidation changes flavor (subtle)
- The fuller the bottle, the slower the change

**Storage**:
- Tight seal
- Upright
- Cool, dark place

**Pro tip**: Transfer to smaller bottles when low (less air exposure)

---

### 🍷 WINE (Still)
**Timeline**: 1-5 days

**Red wine**: 3-5 days
- Heavier reds (Cab, Syrah) last longer
- Light reds (Pinot) fade faster
- Recork, cool/dark place

**White wine**: 3-5 days
- Refrigerate after opening
- Higher acid = lasts longer

**Rosé**: 3-5 days
- Refrigerate

**Why it goes bad**: Oxygen turns wine to vinegar

**Extend life**: Vacuum pump, wine preservation spray (argon gas)

---

### 🍾 WINE (Sparkling)
**Timeline**: 1-3 days

**What happens**: Bubbles go flat (CO₂ escapes)

**Storage**: 
- Special champagne stopper (not cork)
- Refrigerate
- Upright

**Pro tip**: Quality champagne holds bubbles longer than cheap prosecco

---

### 🍷 FORTIFIED WINE (Port, Sherry, Vermouth)
**Timeline**: Varies widely

**Vermouth**: 1-3 months (refrigerate!)
- It's wine-based, needs cold
- Dry vermouth: 1 month
- Sweet vermouth: 2-3 months

**Port**:
- Ruby/Tawny: 4-6 weeks (recork)
- Vintage: 2-3 days (delicate)
- Late Bottled Vintage (LBV): 1 week

**Sherry**:
- Fino/Manzanilla: 1-2 weeks (refrigerate)
- Amontillado/Oloroso: 2-3 weeks
- Cream/Pedro Ximénez: 2-3 months

**Madeira**: Months (high alcohol + acid = stable)

---

### 🍺 BEER (Opened)
**Timeline**: Hours to 1 day

**What happens**: 
- CO₂ escapes (flat beer)
- Oxidation (cardboard flavor)

**Reality**: Drink it same day or pour it out

**Exception**: High ABV stouts (12%+) might be OK next day

---

### 🍯 LIQUEURS
**Timeline**: 6 months to 2 years

**Cream liqueurs** (Baileys, RumChata):
- Refrigerate after opening
- 6 months max
- Check for curdling

**Coffee liqueurs** (Kahlúa): 1-2 years

**Fruit liqueurs**: 6-12 months

**Herbal liqueurs** (Chartreuse, Fernet): 1-2 years (high proof = stable)

**Citrus liqueurs** (Limoncello): 6-12 months (citrus oils fade)

---

### 🍶 SAKE
**Timeline**: 1-2 weeks (refrigerated)

**What happens**: 
- Delicate flavors fade
- Oxidation changes taste

**Storage**: 
- Refrigerate immediately after opening
- Consume within 2 weeks
- Some say 3 days for best quality

**Unopened**: 1 year (refrigerate after purchase)

---

## Special Cases

### 🧊 Can You Freeze Alcohol?

**High proof spirits** (40%+ ABV):
- Won't freeze in home freezer (-4°F / -20°C)
- Vodka/gin in freezer = OK (smoother, viscous)
- Whiskey in freezer = dulls flavor (not recommended)

**Wine/Beer**:
- Will freeze (lower ABV)
- Expands and can break bottle
- Ruins texture/carbonation
- Don't freeze

**Freezing point by ABV**:
- 5% ABV (beer): 27°F
- 12% ABV (wine): 15°F
- 20% ABV (port): 5°F
- 40% ABV (spirits): -10°F
- 80 proof won't freeze in standard freezer

---

### 🔍 How to Tell If It's Bad

**Visual signs**:
- Cloudiness (where it should be clear)
- Sediment (unusual in spirits)
- Color change (browning in white wine)
- Mold (cream liqueurs)
- Separation that won't remix

**Smell test**:
- Vinegar (wine)
- Cardboard/wet paper (oxidized beer)
- Musty/moldy
- Off-putting chemical smell

**Taste**:
- Sour (when it shouldn't be)
- Flat (beer/sparkling)
- Harsh/bitter (beyond normal)

**When in doubt**: If it smells or tastes wrong, dump it

---

### 🧴 Does Alcohol Sanitize Itself?

**Yes, but...**

**High-proof spirits** (40%+ ABV):
- Naturally antimicrobial
- Bacteria/mold can't grow
- Still oxidizes and loses flavor

**Low-proof drinks** (wine, beer, sake):
- Can harbor bacteria
- Can grow mold (if exposed)
- Need proper storage

**Bottom line**: High-proof = safe to drink but might taste bad; Low-proof = can actually spoil

## The Golden Rules

1. **High proof = low risk** — Spirits last almost forever
2. **Wine needs care** — Cool, dark, horizontal (unopened) or fridge (opened)
3. **Vermouth is wine** — Refrigerate after opening (most people forget this!)
4. **Beer is perishable** — Drink fresh, especially IPAs
5. **When in doubt, smell it** — Your nose knows
6. **Air is the enemy** — Less air in bottle = slower degradation
7. **Heat kills flavor** — Never store above 75°F for extended periods
8. **Cream liqueurs expire** — Check dates, refrigerate after opening
